## Cats do not advertise pain

Signs of illness and pain are often hard to see. The life stage guidelines describe the cat as sometimes reclusive or stoic, and they say one reason cats miss veterinary care is that owners believe a cat who looks quiet does not need it. A content cat holds the ears forward, the whiskers loose, and the muscles soft. Distress can be a crouch, freezing, hiding, or flight, with the ears flat or turned and the pupils wide. Those postures are easy to miss if the cat's plan is to stay still.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Introduction][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Feline communication signs]

The 2022 interaction guidelines treat pain as both a sensation and an emotion. Fear-anxiety can make pain matter more to that cat, and pain can increase fear. One response to a threat is inhibition: the cat goes quiet, with the eyes partly closed and the ears turned out. People often do not recognize that response, so the cat does not get relief and may later switch to flight or to biting.[^aafp-isfm-interact-2022|Negative (protective) emotions]

Pain is also overlooked in clinics. The grimace-scale paper notes that cats are prescribed fewer analgesic drugs than dogs, and it lists difficult recognition, limited training, and a shortage of cat-specific tools among the reasons.[^grimace-2019|Introduction]

## The Feline Grimace Scale

The 2019 study built a scale for naturally occurring acute pain. Five action units were kept because they differed between painful cats and control cats: ear position, orbital tightening (the eyes), muzzle tension, whisker change, and head position. Each unit is scored 0 (absent), 1 (moderately present, or the observer is unsure), or 2 (obviously present). The total is the sum divided by the maximum possible score, so the result runs from 0 to 1. If one area cannot be seen, it is left out of both the sum and the maximum.[^grimace-2019|Image scoring and validation of the FGS]

The scale was higher in painful cats than in controls. It tracked another validated acute-pain instrument closely (rho = 0.86). Agreement between raters was good, and the same rater agreed with themselves on a second scoring. After analgesic treatment, scores fell.[^grimace-2019|Abstract]

Row C is the muzzle, from round, to a mild oval, to a flat ellipse. Row D is whiskers, from loose curves, to straighter, to straight and forward. **Figure.** Letters A to E are the five action units kept in the 2019 scale. Columns 0, 1, and 2 are absent, moderately present or uncertain, and obviously present. A, ears: facing forward, slightly pulled apart, rotated outward. B, eyes: opened, partially closed, squinted. C, muzzle: relaxed and round, mild tension, tense and elliptical. D, whiskers: loose and curved, slightly curved or straighter and closer together, straight and moving forward. The dashed line in E is the shoulder line: head above it, head aligned with it, or head below it or tilted down with the chin toward the chest. An action unit that cannot be seen is not scored. This is a schematic, not a photograph from the paper, and it is not a way to choose a drug. [^grimace-2019|Supplementary training manual]

:::evidence-limited

The cut-off chosen for rescue analgesia in that study was a score above 0.39 out of 1.0. It was selected because, against the comparison scale, that threshold had a sensitivity of 90.7% and a specificity of 86.6%. The images came from 35 client-owned cats with acute painful disease and 20 healthy control cats, scored by researchers and veterinary anaesthesiologists, not by owners at home. The paper tests acute pain. It does not show that the same number detects arthritis or another chronic problem, and it does not authorize an owner to give a pain drug. An owner who sees ears held to the side, eyes squeezed, a tense muzzle, whiskers that have changed, or a head held down should contact a veterinarian.[^grimace-2019|Abstract][^grimace-2019|Image scoring and validation of the FGS]

:::

## Changes that are not "just age" or "just personality"

The life stage guidelines ask clinics to teach owners the subtle signs of illness, pain, and anxiety, and to treat a change in normal behavior as significant. In mature adult and senior cats they specifically ask about appetite, drinking more, urinating more, vomiting, vomiting hairballs, and diarrhea. New nighttime activity or vocalization, and a change in habits, may be cognitive dysfunction, pain, reduced vision, hyperthyroidism, hypertension, or declining hearing. A senior cat who stops jumping onto a favorite seat may have degenerative joint disease or muscle loss, not a sudden preference for the floor. Estimates cited there put clinical signs associated with degenerative joint disease at 40% to 92% of cats, more common and more severe with age. Shoulders, hips, elbows, stifles, and hocks are the joints named most often.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Discussion items for all life stages][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Mature adult and senior cats][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Senior cats]

:::evidence-limited

The 40% to 92% range is a spread across published studies, not one count of every cat. It is wide because feline arthritis is hard to diagnose. It is a reason to take a change in jumping seriously. It is not a prediction that this cat has arthritis.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Senior cats]

:::

A cat who stops using the litter box may have urinary disease, constipation, diabetes, pain, or a box that is now too hard to enter. Seek care promptly. The guidelines name urethral blockage as a life-threatening example, and they say delay also lets the behavior become a habit.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|General litter box considerations]

## When to go urgently

- Little or no urine, straining in the box, or crying while trying to pass urine. The life stage guidelines single out urinary blockage as a reason for prompt care.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|General litter box considerations]
- A face that matches several grimace features at once, especially with hiding, not moving, or not eating. The scale was built for acute pain, and scores in the study fell after analgesia.[^grimace-2019|Abstract]
- Drinking more, urinating more, repeated vomiting, or a clear loss of weight, particularly in a cat over 7 years old. These are the history items the guidelines use to decide on blood and urine tests.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Mature adult and senior cats]
- A new inability to jump, or nighttime crying, in an older cat. The guidelines say these are not explained away as ordinary aging.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Senior cats]

<!-- nonclaim --> Open-mouth breathing, collapse, seizures, possible poisoning, and a full day of not eating are on [See a vet now](../urgent/).
