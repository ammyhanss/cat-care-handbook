<!-- nonclaim --> This page is owner education about routine visits. It does not give doses. A veterinarian has not reviewed it. The age bands are a clinical framework. A cat does not change biologically on a birthday.

## Four ages, plus the end of life

The 2021 guidelines use four age-related stages and an end-of-life stage that can happen at any age. A kitten is from birth up to 1 year. A young adult is from 1 year through 6 years. A mature adult is from 7 years through 10 years. A senior is older than 10 years. The task force says any age cut-off is an arbitrary mark on a continuous life. A senior cat who is in excellent condition may still be managed as a mature adult, at the veterinarian's discretion. The bands are a starting point for an individual plan.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 1][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Life stage definitions and relevant clinical perspectives]

Examples of what the bands are meant to highlight: congenital problems in kittens, prevention of obesity in young adults, and earlier attention to kidney disease in mature adult and senior cats.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Life stage definitions and relevant clinical perspectives]

## How often to go

Every cat should be examined at least once a year, and more often if that cat needs it. Senior cats should be seen at least every 6 months, and more often if they have a chronic disease. The guidelines point to the AAFP senior-care guidelines for the detail of that stage. Young adult cats are seen less often than kittens, because the kitten vaccine series creates extra visits, but the examination does not stop. Routine visits are how behavioral and medical change is found before it is severe, painful, or more costly.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Discussion items for all life stages][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Young adult cats]

<!-- nonclaim --> The kitten vaccine series, including the dose around 6 months of age, is on [Vaccination, parasites, and the first visit](./vaccines-and-parasites/).

## What every visit includes

At every life stage the clinic should record weight, body condition, and muscle condition, ask about food and water intake, ask whether any urine or feces has appeared outside the box, and talk about behavior that has changed. Subtle signs of illness, pain, and anxiety are a teaching point, not a footnote. Sterilization, claw care and alternatives to declawing, microchip identification, and carrier practice are on the list. The cat's temperament, and which handling worked, should be written down.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Discussion items for all life stages]

WSAVA asks for a nutritional assessment at every visit, with a feeding recommendation. A body condition below 4 or above 5 on the 9-point scale, muscle loss, an unusual or homemade diet, or treats above 10% of calories are reasons for a longer look at the diet.[^wsava-nutrition-2011|Nutritional evaluation][^wsava-nutrition-2011|Table 1]

Lifestyle is not only "indoor" or "outdoor." The guidelines call that split too simple. The useful questions include whether outdoor time is in an enclosure, on a leash, or free roaming, and whether the cat meets other cats, goes to boarding, or attends a show. A hunting young adult and an indoor mature cat in a quiet home do not get the same preventive plan.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Lifestyle risk assessment]

## What changes with age

Kitten visits look harder at murmurs, hernias, teeth, and infectious disease, and they are the time for socialization to the clinic, handling, and the carrier. Positive reinforcement is the method named. Punishment and aversive handling are to be avoided.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Incorporating kitten socialization into the examination visit]

Young adult visits watch for weight gain and for periodontal disease and tooth resorption. This is also the age when play between cats falls and conflict can appear, including after a stressful event such as a clinic visit.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Intercat relations]

Mature adult and senior visits pay more attention to the mouth, the abdomen, the eyes, the heart and lungs, the joints, the thyroid, and the kidneys, and they include a pain assessment. New behavior is not written off as old age. Nighttime crying, drinking more, and a stop in jumping are reasons to examine, not to wait.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Senior cats]

## Tests on a healthy cat

Table 4 is for apparently healthy cats. It is not the list used before anesthesia. The task force says direct proof that this testing schedule improves outcomes is not available. They still recommend collecting the data, because trends such as a rising creatinine, SDMA, or total thyroxine, a rising blood pressure, or a falling urine specific gravity can be the early sign, and because a baseline makes the next result easier to read.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Recommended diagnostics based on life stage]

Fecal examination is strongly recommended in kittens. A complete blood count, a serum biochemistry panel, urinalysis, total thyroxine, SDMA, and blood pressure are marked "consider" in young adults, "recommended" in mature adults, and "strongly recommended" in seniors. The suggested rhythm is a single baseline, then as needed, for kittens and young adults; every 1 to 2 years for mature adults; and at least yearly for seniors, with every 6 months recommended. Retrovirus testing is listed as something to consider from the young adult stage onward in the way the table is drawn, and the text sends the detailed retrovirus schedule to the 2020 AAFP retrovirus guidelines rather than restating it.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 4]

:::evidence-limited

The table's benefit is the task force's judgment, not a trial that measured years of life gained from these blood tests. Testing frequency also depends on lifestyle, exposure, and geography. A cat with signs does not wait for the healthy-cat interval.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Recommended diagnostics based on life stage][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 4]

## What to bring to a routine visit

- A note of what the cat ate, how much water disappeared, and whether the box was used, since the last visit.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2]
- Any change in jumping, sleeping place, night-time voice, or willingness to be touched.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Senior cats][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Feline communication signs]
- The carrier the cat already sleeps in, if you have started that habit.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Training kittens in preparation to be adult cats]
- Questions about the next interval: yearly, or every 6 months if the cat is over 10 or has a chronic disease.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Discussion items for all life stages]
