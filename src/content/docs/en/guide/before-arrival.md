## What the home has to offer

A cat's comfort in a place is tied to physical health, emotional welfare, and behavior. Meeting environmental needs is essential, not optional, for a cat that will live indoors, outdoors, or both.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Abstract]

The 2013 guidelines group those needs into five pillars. The cat needs a safe place. Key resources (food, water, toileting, scratching, play, and rest) should be available in more than one spot and kept apart from each other. The cat needs a chance to play and to carry out predatory-type behavior. Human contact should be positive, consistent, and predictable. The home should respect the cat's sense of smell.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Five pillars of a healthy feline environment]

Pet cats still use many patterns of the African wildcat. A home is a small territory compared with the range a cat would use outside, so resting places, feeding stations, water, scratching posts, and litter boxes need to be plentiful and spread out.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Behavior and environmental needs]

## A safe place

A safe place is private and secure, and it is often raised. The cat retreats there to feel protected. Not being able to see a threat is enough, even when the whole body is not hidden. When the cat is relaxed, the same spot can be a bed.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1]

A cardboard box on its side, with a roof and an easy opening, is one working example. A perch on that roof lets the cat watch while staying apart. A carrier left out, with the cat's own bedding inside, is a safe place that already smells familiar and is ready for travel. An open wire carrier does not let the cat hide. A blanket over part of it does.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1]

If more than one cat will live in the home, provide at least as many safe places as there are cats, each large enough for one cat, with more than one way in so another cat cannot block the only door. Put those places apart from each other. For a kitten, or an older cat that does not jump well, keep boxes and shelves low, or add a ramp.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1]

A carrier that stays out, and that the cat can enter by choice, is the transportable safe place the environmental guidelines describe. The cat-friendly clinic guidelines ask caregivers to keep an inviting carrier in the home all the time, in a place the cat already likes, rather than producing it only on the morning of a visit.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1][^isfm-aafp-clinic-2022|Preparation to reduce stress prior to the clinic visit]

## Food, water, and a toilet, kept apart

Eating, drinking, toileting, scratching, play, and rest are the key resources. Put each kind in its own place. One cat should have a choice: the guidelines' example is at least two resting areas, two feeding areas, and two toileting areas. Put food and water apart from each other so the cat is not asked to eat beside the water or the toilet.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 2]

Keep a clear distance between toileting areas and the other resources.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 2]

## Scratching and smell

Scratching is normal cat behavior. Posts should be in the home when the cat arrives. Useful places are near a resting spot and along a path the cat will use. In one study, rope was the substrate cats used most often when it was offered, although carpet was offered more often. Cats 10 to 14 years old preferred carpet, and other ages preferred rope. Posts that were used more often were a simple upright, or a cat tree with two or more levels and a height of at least 3 feet, with a base no wider than 3 feet.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Training kittens in preparation to be adult cats]

Scratching also leaves scent from glands in the feet. Give a surface that allows that. Avoid products that wipe out the smell the cat uses to recognize home, including heavily scented litter. Do not wash every bed on the same day. Leave some bedding that still smells of the cat. A new object can be rubbed with a cloth that has picked up the cat's scent during a calm contact.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 5]

## Indoors, outdoors, or both

:::evidence-limited

The 2021 life stage guidelines say there is controversy over indoor-only living versus outdoor access. The debate follows geography, culture, and what the owner prefers. The aim they name is an environment that is stimulating and safe, and they recommend a microchip for every cat. The 2013 environmental guidelines leave the choice with the owner, shaped by local beliefs, local rules, and how safe the place is. They also say that, if possible, a pet cat should be able to reach safe outdoor places, that an enclosure reduces injury and contact with predators and free-roaming cats, and that loose leash walking is another option after positive training. CAPC's general guidelines tell owners to keep cats indoors to limit roaming and hunting. These documents do not agree. The veterinarian who knows the street, the local infections, and this cat is the person to ask.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Intercat relations][^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1][^capc-general-2025|Healthy Lifestyle, Healthy Pets, Healthy People]

:::

## Before the cat comes through the door

- A hiding place with an easy opening, and a carrier that stays out with bedding the cat can use.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 1]
- Food and water in separate places, not beside the toileting area.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 2]
- Two toileting places for one cat, in two locations.[^aafp-isfm-env-2013|Pillar 2]
- A scratching post near rest or a path through the home, in place on day one.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Training kittens in preparation to be adult cats]
- Unscented clumping litter, or the litter the kitten was already using, with other types available so the kitten can show a preference.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2]
- A microchip plan. The life stage guidelines say every cat should be microchipped, and CAPC lists a microchip as permanent identification.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Intercat relations][^capc-general-2025|Healthy Lifestyle, Healthy Pets, Healthy People]
- A veterinary appointment. For a kitten or a newly adopted cat whose medical history is unknown, the life stage guidelines call broad-spectrum parasite treatment, effective against heartworms, intestinal parasites, and fleas, prudent, and they note that other animals in the home may need treatment at the same time.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Parasite control]
