## What the first visits are doing

Vaccination is one part of a preventive plan. The visit should include a physical examination and a conversation about how the clinic judges risk for this cat.[^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Abstract]

For a kitten, the life stage examination pays extra attention to congenital findings such as heart murmurs, hernias, and the teeth, and to signs of infectious disease. Body weight, body condition score, and muscle condition score are recorded at every life stage. The most invasive parts of the exam, such as the mouth, temperature, nail trim, and sample collection, are kept until the end.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Importance of feline-friendly handling]

Kittens do not stay on an annual schedule. Young adult cats need less frequent routine care than kittens, and the life stage guidelines still want the owner to understand why examinations continue.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Young adult cats]

## Core vaccines, and why the calendar is not universal

:::evidence-limited

WSAVA states that its vaccination guidelines are broad on purpose. A schedule that fits one country can fit another country poorly, and national associations are expected to build a local schedule. WSAVA defines core cat vaccines, for every cat, as those against feline parvovirus, feline calicivirus, and feline herpesvirus-1. Rabies vaccine is core where rabies is endemic, even if local law does not require it. In places where feline leukemia virus disease is endemic, FeLV vaccine is core for cats under 1 year of age and for adult cats with outdoor access or who live with cats that go outdoors. The 2020 AAHA/AAFP guidelines, written for a North American context, list FHV-1, FCV, FPV, rabies, and FeLV in cats younger than 1 year as core. FeLV in cats older than 1 year is non-core and depends on exposure. Chlamydia felis and Bordetella bronchiseptica vaccines are non-core. Both organizations tell the veterinarian to follow the vaccine label and local law, especially for rabies. The interval after the kitten series is not one number worldwide. Use the schedule your veterinarian sets for your region.[^wsava-vax-2024|Executive summary][^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Vaccination Principles][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Vaccination]

:::

The shared kitten plan is still specific. Maternally derived antibody blocks many core vaccines, and the amount of that antibody differs between litters and between kittens in one litter, so neither group can name one date when every kitten will respond. WSAVA recommends core doses every 2 to 4 weeks, with the last dose at 16 weeks of age or older. If a kitten can only be vaccinated once, that dose should be at 16 weeks or older. A further dose at or after 26 weeks, rather than waiting until 12 to 16 months, is advised so the minority of kittens who still had interfering antibody at 16 weeks are not left unprotected for a year. The 2020 AAHA/AAFP task force adopted revaccination against FPV, FHV-1, and FCV at 6 months of age for the same reason, and it notes that the kitten series is often carried through 16 to 18 weeks. An interval shorter than 2 weeks during the series can interfere with the response.[^wsava-vax-2024|Executive summary][^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Vaccination Principles]

The life stage guidelines recommend FeLV revaccination 12 months after the last kitten dose, and then annually for the individual cat who is at high risk. They also say core vaccines should be given to every cat, and non-core vaccines no more often than risk requires.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Vaccination]

WSAVA notes that the duration of immunity from most modern modified-live core vaccines is many years, and that a guideline may recommend revaccination every 3 years, or less often, for a product whose label still says 1 year. Antibody testing can inform decisions about some core antigens, including FPV, in adult cats. That is a clinic decision, not a home test.[^wsava-vax-2024|Executive summary][^wsava-vax-2024|Introduction]

Protection from a vaccine is not absolute. FPV vaccines tend to protect more reliably than FHV-1 vaccines. Reducing exposure still matters.[^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Vaccination Principles]

## A lump after a vaccine

Feline injection-site sarcoma is uncommon, and estimates of how often it occurs vary by country and by which vaccines are used. It is aggressive and locally invasive. The task force says not to inject between the shoulder blades, and not to rely on using a smaller volume. Injections in the distal limb make amputation with margins possible if a sarcoma forms. The life stage guidelines specify below the elbow or stifle, or the distal third of the tail, and they say the record should include the site and the route.[^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Postvaccination Monitoring][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Vaccination]

Ask the clinic to show you the spot. The 2020 guidelines tell veterinarians and owners to watch it with the "3-2-1" rule. A mass should be biopsied if it is still there 3 months after vaccination, if it is larger than 2 cm across, or if it is still growing 1 month after vaccination. Any one of those three is enough.[^aaha-aafp-vax-2020|Postvaccination Monitoring]

## Parasites: two councils, not one calendar

Even a cat who lives only indoors has a real risk of parasites. The life stage guidelines recommend year-round broad-spectrum products with efficacy against heartworms, intestinal parasites, and fleas for every patient, indoor or outdoor, and tick control when the risk assessment says so. Fecal examination is used as appropriate. A negative fecal test does not rule out infection. For a kitten or a newly adopted cat with an unknown history, they call that broad-spectrum treatment prudent, and other animals in the home may need to be treated at the same time because roundworms and fleas can pass between them. Outdoor lifestyle, geography, and time in boarding or at a groomer can raise the risk further. Heartworm prevention is preferred because diagnosis in cats is difficult and treatment carries risk.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Table 2][^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Parasite control]

CAPC's 2025 general guidelines also call for year-round broad-spectrum control against heartworm, intestinal parasites, fleas, and ticks, because some of these parasites can infect people. They ask for fecal examinations at least four times in the first year of life, and at least twice a year in healthy adults, with the adult frequency adjusted for health, outdoor access, travel, and contact with other animals. They say passive fecal flotation is not the recommended method. They also say to feed commercial or cooked food rather than raw diets, to wash hands after contact with feces, and not to handle feces or urine with bare hands. That last point is especially important for children and people at higher risk.[^capc-general-2025|Parasite Testing and Protection Guided by Veterinarians][^capc-general-2025|Every Pet, All Year Long][^capc-general-2025|Healthy Lifestyle, Healthy Pets, Healthy People]

:::evidence-limited

ESCCAP's European worm guideline does not use one interval for every cat. It groups animals by exposure. Additional cat guidance starts roundworm control at 3 weeks of age and describes a repeated plan through weaning, then a different plan if the kitten keeps roaming. Cats who hunt, eat prey or raw meat, or live with children under about 5 to 6 years or with people who are immunocompromised are placed in higher-frequency groups. Where the concern is Toxocara and the dog or cat is housed outside or has outdoor access, ESCCAP recommends deworming at least four times a year if fecal examinations are not used instead. These frequencies are the council's scheme for a veterinarian in Europe. They are not the same as CAPC's year-round broad-spectrum recommendation, and they are not a dose. The product and the interval belong to the veterinarian who knows the local parasites.[^esccap-gl1-2025|Additional treatments for cats][^esccap-gl1-2025|Lifelong control of common worms]

:::

People with healthy immune systems are at very low risk of catching a zoonosis from a healthy cat. The risk is higher for older adults, children younger than 5 years, pregnant people, and people who are immunosuppressed. Parasite control and flea control are part of protecting both the cat and the people in the house. The life stage guidelines also say not to feed raw or undercooked meat, because pet food of that kind can carry zoonotic agents.[^aaha-aafp-life-2021|Zoonoses and human safety]
