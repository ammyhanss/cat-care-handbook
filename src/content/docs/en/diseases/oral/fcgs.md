## In one sentence

Feline chronic gingivostomatitis is severe, long-lasting inflammation of the oral mucosa. The usual site is beside the palatoglossal folds, toward the back of the mouth, and the lesions may be ulcerative, proliferative, or both.[^lee-2020|Introduction] The 2023 clinical review describes the same pattern: inflammation of the gingiva that extends onto the alveolar and buccal mucosa, with moderate to severe pain.[^soltero-2023|Introduction]

## Common signs

Affected cats often eat less or stop eating, groom poorly, and interact less with people.[^soltero-2023|Introduction] Drooling, nausea, and a poor appetite also occur, and those signs can overlap with esophagitis when the two are present together.[^lee-2020|Feline Chronic Gingivostomatitis and Esophagitis] Ulcerated areas can bleed.[^soltero-2023|Figure 1] In some cats the proliferative tissue is severe enough that the tongue cannot sit back in its usual position.[^lee-2020|Figure 2] The usual inflammation sits deep in the mouth, lateral to the palatoglossal folds.[^lee-2020|Introduction]

Reported prevalence depends on the cats studied and on how the disease is defined. A 2020 review gives a range of 0.7% to 12%.[^lee-2020|Introduction] A 2023 review states a figure of up to 26%.[^soltero-2023|Introduction] Cats in shared homes have higher odds: one study summarized in the 2023 review found about a seven-fold increase compared with single-cat homes, and each additional cat raised the odds by more than 70%.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS] Outdoor access, in the study cited by the 2020 review, was not associated with the disease.[^lee-2020|Environmental Stressors]

No single cause has been proved. Feline calicivirus is the agent with the most consistent association.[^lee-2020|Potential Viral Causes] In one metagenomic study summarized in 2023, calicivirus was found in 21 of 23 affected cats and in none of the control cats, but inoculating healthy cats did not reproduce the chronic disease.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS] Oral viral load has not been shown to match how severe the lesions are or how the cat responds to treatment.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS] Feline herpesvirus, feline immunodeficiency virus, feline leukemia virus, and several bacteria have been discussed without proof that any one of them is the cause.[^lee-2020|Etiopathogenesis]

:::evidence-limited

The causal role of calicivirus is not settled. The reviews support an association and a possible effect on severity. They do not show that the virus, by itself, produces this chronic disease in every infected cat.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS][^lee-2020|Potential Viral Causes]

:::

In one controlled study, endoscopy found esophagitis in 98% of cats with this disease and in none of the control cats. The cats often had no signs that pointed to the esophagus, and the inflammation improved in cats whose mouth disease then came under control.[^soltero-2023|Systemic manifestations][^lee-2020|Feline Chronic Gingivostomatitis and Esophagitis]

## When to see a vet immediately

Reduced or absent appetite and marked mouth pain are reasons to see a veterinarian promptly. Cats are sometimes euthanized when the pain is not controlled or when treatment cannot be obtained.[^soltero-2023|Introduction]

- Bleeding from ulcerated mouth tissue is part of the described picture and should be examined.[^soltero-2023|Figure 1]
- Proliferative inflammation that stops the tongue from resting normally needs a veterinary examination.[^lee-2020|Figure 2]
- A cat that has stopped eating needs assessment, because loss of appetite is a typical effect of the pain.[^soltero-2023|Introduction]

## Tests the clinic may use

An awake look at the mouth is often not enough. The veterinarian may need sedation, or may wait until anesthesia, to see the lesions and to complete the scoring used to follow the disease.[^soltero-2023|Preoperative evaluation] Full-mouth dental radiographs are part of evaluation. Cats with this disease have generalized, advanced periodontitis and are more likely to have external inflammatory root resorption than cats with other oral disease.[^lee-2020|Feline Chronic Gingivostomatitis and Periodontitis] The 2023 review, citing earlier case series, states that moderate to severe periodontitis is present in up to 93% of affected cats, and tooth resorption or retained roots in up to 66%.[^soltero-2023|Surgical management]

Before a long anesthetic procedure for extractions, the clinic should assess anesthetic risk, including a complete blood count and a serum biochemistry panel.[^soltero-2023|Preoperative evaluation] Testing for feline leukemia virus, feline immunodeficiency virus, and feline calicivirus is recommended because virus status can change the outlook. To the authors of the 2023 review, a commercial test for puma feline foamy virus was not available.[^soltero-2023|Preoperative evaluation] A positive virus test does not, by itself, establish this diagnosis. The agents have been linked without proof of causation, although feline leukemia virus has a reported effect on outcome: in one series, positive cats had a 7.5-times greater chance of no improvement after extractions.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS][^lee-2020|Etiopathogenesis]

The diagnosis is confirmed by an incisional biopsy under anesthesia.[^soltero-2023|Preoperative evaluation] Affected tissue contains large numbers of lymphocytes and plasma cells, with fewer neutrophils, Mott cells, and mast cells.[^lee-2020|Etiopathogenesis] Where the lesions sit, lateral to the palatoglossal folds, remains central to recognizing the disease in the clinic.[^lee-2020|Introduction]

## Treatment approaches

<!-- nonclaim --> This page does not give doses, dosing intervals, or course lengths. Which drug to use is a decision for the veterinarian who has examined the cat.

Pain control is required whatever else is done. The choice depends on other diseases, other drugs, and how painful the mouth is.[^lee-2020|Pain Management] The 2023 review treats analgesia as necessary before surgery, after surgery, and in cats who remain inflamed.[^soltero-2023|Analgesia]

Medical treatment alone is not a substitute for extraction. The 2020 review states that medical treatment by itself usually does not give a favorable long-term result, and that extraction of at least the premolar and molar teeth, with or without medicine, is the standard direction.[^lee-2020|Key points][^lee-2020|Leading treatment modalities] The 2023 review is explicit that medicine given before extraction has not been shown to improve the surgical result, and that medicine has no role as the only treatment in place of surgery.[^soltero-2023|Medical management]

In the 95-cat extraction series, as reported by the 2020 review, 28.4% of cats had complete resolution, 39% had substantial improvement, 26.3% had little improvement, and 6.3% had none. Of the cats with substantial improvement or complete resolution, 68.8% still needed antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, or analgesic medicine for a finite time after the first two postoperative weeks.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment] The same series found no significant difference in overall response between premolar-and-molar extraction and full-mouth extraction.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment][^winer-2016|Discussion] The 2020 review's practical suggestion is to extract the premolars and molars first, plus any tooth that already needs extraction because of severe periodontitis, a retained root, or resorption, and to consider full-mouth extraction if there is no positive response within 1 to 4 months.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment] The 2023 review's key points differ in emphasis: full-mouth extraction when inflammation reaches the front of the mouth, and premolar-and-molar extraction when inflammation is limited to those teeth.[^soltero-2023|Key Points]

:::evidence-limited

Percentages from different extraction studies should not be added together. The 2016 systematic review found that scoring systems and follow-up times differ, so a direct comparison of success rates is unreliable. Smaller early series looked better than the 95-cat series, which is the largest of the extraction reports in that review.[^winer-2016|Abstract][^winer-2016|Inconsistent Outcome Measurements]

:::

:::evidence-limited

Evidence for antibiotics is limited. One study summarized in the 2023 review reported improvement in 38% of cats given amoxicillin and 37% given metronidazole, and those effects were temporary. The review recommends antibiotics only in the acute setting or when a secondary infection is present, not as a cure.[^soltero-2023|Antimicrobials]

:::

Glucocorticoids are often prescribed, but the 2023 review reports complete cure or marked improvement in about 23% of patients in the series it cites, and clinical remission in only 7% of that improved group. Long-term use can cause harm, including diabetes mellitus. The authors reserve corticosteroids for cats who do not respond to pain control, for a tapering course, or as a salvage option, and they advise against immunosuppression without surgery.[^soltero-2023|Immunosuppressive therapy]

For cats who remain affected after extraction, a small randomized trial of oral ciclosporin found improvement in 7 of 9 treated cats over 6 weeks, compared with 1 of 7 cats given placebo. In longer observation of 11 cats, 5 were clinically cured after 3 or more months.[^soltero-2023|Immunosuppressive therapy][^winer-2016|Medical Management] The 2016 review also reports the interferon comparison: oromucosal recombinant feline interferon omega produced substantial improvement in 45% of 19 cats, including remission in 10%, against substantial improvement in 23% of 11 cats given a tapering glucocorticoid, including remission in 7%. The difference between those groups was not statistically significant.[^winer-2016|Medical Management] The 2020 review reads that result as interferon being at least as effective as short-term prednisolone in calicivirus-positive cats who were already refractory to extraction.[^lee-2020|Recombinant feline interferon omega] The product is approved in Europe and, at the time of the 2023 review, was not approved in the United States.[^soltero-2023|Immunomodulation]

:::evidence-limited

Those medicine trials are small. A result in 9 or 19 cats does not establish how an individual cat will respond, and the interferon comparison did not show a statistically significant difference from the glucocorticoid arm.[^winer-2016|Medical Management]

:::

Intravenous adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells have been studied in refractory cats. In one series of 7 cats given their own cells, 42.8% went into clinical remission, 28.6% improved substantially, and 28.6% did not respond, over 6 to 24 months. Allogeneic cells were less effective in a later series of the same size.[^lee-2020|Mesenchymal stem cells] A pilot study found no substantial benefit when stem cells were given before extraction.[^soltero-2023|Immunomodulation] This remains a referral or research option for cats who have already had appropriate extractions, not the first treatment.[^lee-2020|Summary]

## Care at home

Keep a record of how much the cat eats, whether grooming has stopped, and whether body weight is falling. Those are the changes the disease produces.[^soltero-2023|Introduction] Give only medicine the veterinarian has prescribed. Pain control has to be matched to the cat's other diseases and other drugs.[^lee-2020|Pain Management] After extraction, plan on a recheck. In the 95-cat series, most cats who improved still needed medicine beyond the first two weeks.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment]

In a shared home, the odds of this disease are higher, and the proposed reasons include repeated exposure to viruses shed by other cats and the stress of group housing.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS][^lee-2020|Environmental Stressors] Higher odds are not the same as every cat in the house developing the disease. Experimental inoculation did not turn healthy cats into chronic cases.[^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS]

## Outlook and long-term care

This is a chronic inflammatory disease. Across extraction studies, the 2020 review describes substantial improvement or resolution in about 70% to 80% of cats and little or no improvement in about 20% to 30%, with the caveat from the systematic review that those figures come from studies that were not scored in the same way.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment][^winer-2016|Inconsistent Outcome Measurements] The 2020 summary divides outcomes after surgery into approximate thirds: remission, substantial improvement, and little or no improvement. Many cats need medicine as well as extraction, and some need it long term.[^lee-2020|Summary] Cats who carry feline leukemia virus have a worse reported chance of improvement after extraction.[^soltero-2023|Surgical management] If pain remains uncontrolled, the decision about quality of life is made with the veterinarian. The 2023 review notes that some cats are euthanized for that reason.[^soltero-2023|Introduction]

No study cited in the 2023 review has tested whether a longer history of signs, before surgery, changes the surgical result. Duration of signs was not correlated with lesion scores at the time of treatment in one study the review discusses.[^soltero-2023|Surgical management]

## Common misunderstandings

- "The front gums are only a little red, so it can wait." The defining inflammation is caudal, lateral to the palatoglossal folds, and it is painful even when the owner cannot see it well.[^lee-2020|Introduction][^soltero-2023|Introduction]
- "Antibiotics will clear it." The published antibiotic responses were temporary, and medicine alone is not the treatment in place of extraction.[^soltero-2023|Antimicrobials][^soltero-2023|Medical management]
- "Pull only the worst-looking tooth." The studied surgical approach removes the premolars and molars in the inflamed region, or the full dentition when inflammation is more widespread, plus teeth that already have another reason to be extracted.[^lee-2020|Surgical Treatment][^soltero-2023|Key Points]
- "A steroid injection is a plan by itself." Remission on glucocorticoids was uncommon in the series the 2023 review cites, long-term use can cause diabetes, and the authors advise against immunosuppression without surgery.[^soltero-2023|Immunosuppressive therapy]
- "This is the same disease as tooth resorption." Resorption and periodontitis are common in the same cats. They are findings that change the extraction plan. They are not the definition of this mucosal disease.[^lee-2020|Feline Chronic Gingivostomatitis and Periodontitis][^soltero-2023|Surgical management]
- "A positive calicivirus test makes the diagnosis." Calicivirus is associated with the disease and is also found in cats who do not have it. Inoculation has not proved causation.[^lee-2020|Potential Viral Causes][^soltero-2023|Current knowledge of the etiology of FCGS]
