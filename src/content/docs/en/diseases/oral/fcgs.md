## In one sentence

Feline chronic gingivostomatitis is long-lasting, painful inflammation of the mouth, typically on the mucosa beside and behind the back teeth. The immune response to ongoing stimuli such as dental plaque is out of proportion. It is not ordinary tartar, and a few days of antibiotics do not finish it.

## Common signs

Owners usually notice bad breath, drooling that may be blood-tinged, slow eating, dropped food, a switch to soft food, or a cat that walks up to the bowl and leaves. Appetite and body weight often fall. Some cats paw at the mouth or cry when they yawn. The coat becomes unkempt because grooming hurts.

When the mouth is opened, the gums and the mucosa toward the throat are red, ulcerated, or proliferative. That area is deep in the mouth. Owners usually cannot see it. Periodontal disease and tooth resorption can be present at the same time. They are different problems.

Reported prevalence runs from under 1 percent to about 12 percent, depending on the population and the definition. Multi-cat homes are over-represented. There is no single proven cause. Feline calicivirus is often detected. A positive test does not diagnose this disease, and a negative test does not rule it out. FIV, FeLV, herpesvirus, and some bacteria have all been discussed. None has been shown to be the sole cause.

## When to see a vet immediately

These need a veterinarian the same day:

- Almost no food for about 24 hours.
- Marked lethargy, hiding, or signs of dehydration such as tacky gums or skin that stays tented.
- Bleeding from the mouth that does not stop, or sudden swelling of the face.
- Labored breathing, or a sudden inability to open or close the mouth.

Even if the cat is still eating a little, obvious mouth pain should be booked promptly. Pain reduces intake, and the problem becomes harder to manage.

## Tests the clinic may use

A conscious look is often incomplete. A reliable oral examination usually needs sedation or anesthesia, a dental chart, and full-mouth dental radiographs. Radiographs look for retained roots and tooth resorption. Roots left behind can keep the inflammation going.

Testing for FIV and FeLV is appropriate. A positive result does not diagnose gingivostomatitis. It does change the overall health picture and the cautions around some drugs.

A biopsy is warranted when a lesion is one-sided, mass-like, or unlike the usual bilateral caudal stomatitis, so that squamous cell carcinoma and other diseases are not missed. Histology of chronic gingivostomatitis often shows lymphocytes and plasma cells. That supports chronic inflammation. The diagnosis still rests mainly on where the lesions are and how the cat presents.

The 2019 AAHA Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats treat an oral examination, dental imaging, and proper pain control as part of dentistry, not optional extras.

## Treatment approaches

This page does not give prescription doses. Which drug to use, and for how long, is a decision for the veterinarian who has examined that cat.

A professional dental cleaning alone usually does not control established disease. The most consistent direction in the literature is extraction of the premolars and molars in the inflamed areas, plus any other tooth that already needs extraction because of severe periodontal disease, resorption, or a retained root. The aim is to remove tooth surfaces that keep collecting plaque.

Jennings and colleagues reviewed 95 cats treated with extractions. About 28 percent had complete resolution, about 39 percent had substantial improvement, about 26 percent had little improvement, and about 6 percent had none. In that series, extracting premolars and molars did not differ overall from full-mouth extraction. Most of the cats who improved substantially or resolved still needed medication for a period after surgery.

Veterinarians therefore often start with an adequately extensive extraction and decide at recheck whether the remaining teeth should come out. The review by Lee, Verstraete, and Arzi describes extraction of at least the premolars and molars, rather than medication alone, as the standard direction at that time. Across the studies they summarize, roughly 70 to 80 percent of cats improved substantially, and about 20 to 30 percent did not. Definitions differ. The systematic review by Winer, Arzi, and Verstraete also warns that scoring and follow-up are inconsistent, so the percentages cannot simply be added together.

Pain control before and after surgery is part of treatment. Antibiotics are sometimes used for a short time around surgery for secondary infection. They are not a cure.

Cats who remain painful and inflamed after extraction may be offered an immunomodulatory drug such as cyclosporine, with bloodwork to monitor it. Hennet and colleagues randomized 39 calicivirus-positive cats who still had caudal stomatitis after extractions, comparing oromucosal recombinant feline interferon omega with an oral glucocorticoid. Lesion scores improved in the interferon group. The study was small, most comparisons between the groups were not significantly different, and the product is not available everywhere.

Repeated steroid injections can make the redness look quieter while pain and adverse effects remain. They do not replace extraction. Regenerative approaches such as mesenchymal stem cells are research or referral options, not the first step in general practice.

Do not use human painkillers. Ibuprofen and acetaminophen are poisonous to cats. Essential oils and internet remedies do not replace an oral examination.

## Care at home

Offer soft food the cat will actually eat. Do not force kibble that hurts to chew. During a flare, watch intake, weight, and water every day. Give only what the veterinarian prescribed, and do not adjust it yourself.

Keep the recheck after surgery. Pain control and healing matter as much as which teeth were removed. Tooth brushing or a rinse is sometimes useful in other dental disease. During active stomatitis the mouth is too painful. Ask before you try.

Calicivirus can spread between cats. Gingivostomatitis is not a disease that every exposed cat develops. Have a new cat examined before it joins the household. In a multi-cat home, reduce crowding and stress where you can.

## Outlook and long-term care

This is a chronic disease. Many cats eat and groom again after extractions that are extensive enough and checked with radiographs for retained roots. Some need further medication. A smaller number stay painful and need a specialist. If pain cannot be controlled, quality of life has to be discussed with the veterinarian.

An early, complete assessment and appropriate extractions usually do more for comfort than repeated courses of antibiotics and steroids. Improvement can take weeks to months. It is not finished the day after surgery.

## Common misunderstandings

- "The gums are only a bit red, so it can wait." Inflammation at the back of the mouth can be severely painful even when the cat still approaches food.
- "Antibiotics will clear it." Bacteria are only part of the stimulus. Inflammation often returns when the course ends.
- "Pull only the worst-looking teeth." Plaque on the teeth that remain can keep the disease going.
- "A steroid injection every so often is a plan." It can hide the problem and cause harm.
- "This is the same as tooth resorption." The two diseases can occur together. They are not the same disease.
