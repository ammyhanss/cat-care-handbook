## In one sentence

Feline upper respiratory disease is a set of clinical findings. It can include ocular and nasal discharge ranging from serous to mucopurulent, nosebleeds, sneezing, and conjunctivitis. Signs can be acute (within 10 days) or chronic (more than 10 days). The term "upper respiratory infection" is kept for cats whose findings are already linked to a known virus, bacterium, or fungus. Most cats with an acute presentation are thought to be related to feline herpesvirus or calicivirus, and a portion of them then have a bacterial infection.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Definitions and Causes]

The main host of feline herpesvirus is the domestic cat. Infection is very common, especially in large populations and in shelters. It causes acute upper respiratory and ocular disease, and it is especially severe in kittens. Dendritic ulcers of the cornea are regarded as a specific finding of ocular infection. After the acute phase, almost all cats enter latent infection. It can reactivate later, mainly as conjunctivitis and keratitis, and it can also be entirely without signs.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points] Calicivirus is common in domestic cats and highly contagious. Most infected cats have painful erosions in the mouth plus mild upper respiratory disease. It can also be associated with chronic gingivostomatitis or with limping syndrome. Kittens can develop pneumonia, and highly virulent strains can cause systemic disease with a high mortality.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Key points]

## Common signs

The typical sequence for herpesvirus is drooling and sneezing first, then fever, lethargy, and not eating, together with serous or blood-tinged discharge from the eyes and nose and reddening of the conjunctiva. When a secondary bacterial infection occurs, the discharge becomes purulent. Oral ulcers and skin ulcers are less common. An association with neurologic signs is very uncommon.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Clinical signs] The main findings of acute disease (within 10 days) are sneezing, nasal discharge, drooling, coughing, reddening of the conjunctiva, and serous ocular discharge. Chronic disease can be stromal keratitis, with corneal edema, vessels growing in, and even blindness, or chronic sneezing and nasal discharge. Chronic rhinitis is attributed to herpesvirus only after other pathogens present at the same time have been excluded.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Table 1. FHV disease, lesions and clinical signs]

Acute calicivirus disease is seen mainly in kittens. The incubation period is 2 to 10 days. The main findings are oral ulcers, sneezing, and serous nasal discharge, and fever is also common. Because ulcers on the tongue are very painful, not eating and drooling are often more obvious than the signs of rhinitis. Many cats improve within a few days on supportive treatment alone. When it is severe there can be pneumonia, shown as difficulty breathing, coughing, fever, and depression, especially in kittens.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Acute oral and upper respiratory tract disease] Acute shifting-leg lameness together with fever can be associated with infection, and it can also occur after vaccination. In natural infection, the lameness can appear days to weeks after the oral or respiratory signs, it can be severe, and it can move quickly from one leg to another. Recovery is usually within 24 to 48 hours, and treatment is not necessarily required.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Limping syndrome]

Virulent systemic calicivirus differs from ordinary strains. It causes severe systemic inflammation, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and multiple organ failure, and death is the usual result. Reported mortality is about 30% to 70%. The characteristic findings are edema of the skin, and ulcers of the skin and the paws. The edema is mainly on the head and the limbs. Some cats are jaundiced, and some have severe respiratory distress from pulmonary edema. Outbreaks usually start in multi-cat settings such as shelters or veterinary hospitals. Not every infected cat shows every one of the typical signs.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Virulent systemic feline calicivirus infection] Genetic testing cannot, by itself, confirm or exclude virulent systemic disease. The clinical findings, and whether the virus is spreading, also have to be taken into account.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Virulent systemic feline calicivirus infection]

## When to see a vet immediately

- Open-mouth breathing, labored breathing at rest, or a kitten with coughing, fever, and marked depression. Pneumonia from calicivirus presents as difficulty breathing, and kittens are especially susceptible; virulent systemic infection can cause pulmonary edema and severe respiratory distress.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Acute oral and upper respiratory tract disease][^abcd-fcv-2025|Virulent systemic feline calicivirus infection]
- About three days without any food intake. The herpesvirus guideline writes placement of a feeding tube and enteral nutrition as what is needed when a cat has not eaten for three days or longer. Nasal congestion keeps the cat from smelling food, and oral ulcers make eating painful.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Symptomatic treatment]
- Sudden swelling of the head or the limbs, together with ulcers of the skin or the paws and marked lethargy. This is characteristic of virulent systemic calicivirus. Mortality is high, and it is often linked to an outbreak in a multi-cat setting.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Virulent systemic feline calicivirus infection]
- A painful eye, an eye held closed, or an ulcer on the cornea. Dendritic ulcers are regarded as a specific finding of ocular herpesvirus infection.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points][^abcd-fhv-2026|Clinical signs]

A cat who is only sneezing, still eating, and mentally normal does not necessarily need emergency care the same day. If the nasal discharge is mucopurulent but appetite and demeanor are both normal, some cats resolve on their own within 10 days.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Suspected Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection] Past this acute time course, or when a drug has already been used without improvement, the cat should be examined again, rather than continuing to switch antibiotics.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Chronic Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (>10 Days of Duration)]

## Tests the clinic may use

For a cat who is acute, within 10 days, the history asks about vaccines, contact with other cats, whether the cat goes out, whether the cat has been to a shelter or a hospital, and whether cats or people in the household are ill. Cytology of nasal discharge is of limited help in choosing an antibiotic. For discharge that is serous, with no mucopurulent component, the working group considers that an antibiotic is not needed, because it is more like uncomplicated viral infection.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (≤10 Days Duration)] Aerobic culture of nasal discharge is hard to interpret: some pathogens do not grow on ordinary media, and what does grow can be normal resident bacteria. The working group does not recommend this kind of culture of nasal discharge to guide drug use in acute cases. Molecular tests for herpesvirus, calicivirus, Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia can also be positive in healthy cats, and vaccine strains can be detected, so a positive result in a single individual is hard to interpret.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (≤10 Days Duration)]

The preferred test for herpesvirus is PCR. Taking samples from the conjunctiva, the oropharynx, and the nasal cavity at the same time can raise detection. Antibody cannot be used to predict whether there is protection.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points] Calicivirus is best tested by reverse-transcription PCR, but a negative result cannot exclude it, and healthy cats can also be positive.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Key points] Cats without signs, and cats who have been given a modified-live vaccine, occasionally shed calicivirus, so a positive PCR correlates poorly with the clinical signs of the moment.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Diagnosis]

Beyond 10 days, especially after acute management has failed, a more complete workup looks for other infection, allergy, neoplasia, a foreign body, nasopharyngeal stenosis, an oronasal fistula, a nasopharyngeal polyp, and trauma. Referral is recommended when advanced imaging or nasal endoscopy is not available.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Chronic Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (>10 Days of Duration)] Imaging and endoscopy for chronic rhinitis are done under anesthesia, to separate inflammation from neoplasia. A one-sided lesion is more often seen with neoplasia, but biopsy is still recommended.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Diagnostic imaging (may be performed in chronic rhinitis cases)]

## Treatment approaches

<!-- nonclaim --> This page does not give doses, dosing intervals, or course lengths.

Severely ill cats need nursing and supportive treatment: dehydration and electrolytes are corrected, and the cat is gotten to the point of being able to eat. Food can be mashed and warmed slightly to increase the smell. If the cat has not eaten for three days, a nasogastric tube or an esophagostomy tube is considered. Nasal discharge is cleared with physiologic saline, and a barrier ointment can be put on the skin around the nose. Severe nasal congestion can be treated by nebulizing physiologic saline. When there is no nebulizer, the guideline describes the owner carefully staying with the cat in a bathroom that has hot water, with fog on the mirror meaning the humidity is enough. A nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug is considered only after dehydration has already been corrected, and preferably after the cat is already eating. Maropitant is sometimes used for upper respiratory signs, but no study has shown it to be effective for herpesvirus, and the guideline states that there is no indication for its use. Vitamins A, C, and B12 have no scientific evidence.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Symptomatic treatment]

An antibiotic is not the treatment for every cat who sneezes. When the discharge is serous, use is not recommended. A cat with mucopurulent nasal discharge whose appetite and demeanor are normal can go without one first, during a 10-day period of observation. An antibiotic is considered during that observation period only if fever, lethargy, or not eating is present at the same time.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (≤10 Days Duration)][^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Suspected Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection] If the working group decides to use a drug, the first line is doxycycline or amoxicillin. Fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins are reserved for situations in which culture shows they are needed and doxycycline and amoxicillin cannot be used. The most suitable length of treatment is not known.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Suspected Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection] The herpesvirus guideline treats that same ISCAID recommendation as the direction when the cat is severely ill and a secondary bacterial infection is suspected.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Symptomatic treatment] Drugs for chronic bacterial upper respiratory infection are chosen according to culture and susceptibility. There is no evidence for topical antibacterial drops. Topical physiologic saline is considered to have a mild mucolytic effect.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Chronic Feline Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection]

For acute ocular herpesvirus disease, the antiviral drugs the guideline prefers are topical trifluridine and systemic famciclovir. Other antiviral drugs also exist.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points]

:::evidence-limited

Lysine was once proposed as a way to reduce re-shedding of herpesvirus. Several studies and one meta-analysis did not show it to be effective, and the guideline does not recommend it for preventing re-shedding.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Treatment]

:::

Cats in whom a complete workup shows only lymphoplasmacytic or mixed inflammation, and no cause is found, are called idiopathic rhinosinusitis. Chronic viral infection is speculated to be involved. The true cause remains unclear.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Chronic Feline Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection]

## Care at home

Clear the discharge from the nose and the eyes, offer food with a stronger smell that hurts less to eat, and record whether the cat is eating. If the cat does not eat for three days, go back to discuss a feeding tube. Do not keep waiting.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Symptomatic treatment] Bathroom steam has to leave the cat a way to get out. Do not shut the cat into an overheated space. What the guideline describes is a person and the cat staying together until the mirror fogs.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Symptomatic treatment]

After the acute phase, herpesvirus lies latent in the trigeminal ganglion. Giving birth, lactation, and a move to a new environment can all prompt reactivation. At reactivation there can be signs, or there can be shedding only.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Pathogenesis] Calicivirus is especially troublesome where there are many cats and hygiene is poor. Keeping a stable small group of no more than three helps reduce the problem. It resists many disinfectants. The guideline names disinfectants based on sodium hypochlorite, potassium peroxymonosulfate, and chlorine dioxide.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Key points]

Vaccines against both viruses are listed as core vaccines. For herpesvirus there are only injectable modified-live and killed vaccines. No difference has been written down in how well they protect against disease, and both are made together with calicivirus.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points] A calicivirus vaccine, in most situations, protects cats well against developing acute oral and upper respiratory disease, but it cannot stop infection, and it cannot stop shedding after infection. No vaccine gives equal protection against every field strain. When a cat who was vaccinated completely on schedule still has repeated disease, a change to a vaccine that uses a different strain can be considered. Immunity after recovery is not lifelong, and it does not cover every strain, so vaccination is still recommended for a cat who has recovered and is already healthy.[^abcd-fcv-2025|General recommendations on vaccine type and vaccination protocol][^abcd-fcv-2025|Different FCV strains] In many outbreaks of virulent systemic disease, vaccine did not protect the cats on the scene; experimentally, some studies saw milder disease.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Vaccination and VS-FC infection]

:::evidence-limited

Some studies discuss an association between injectable vaccines and antibodies against feline kidney cells. Those cats did not develop clinically meaningful inflammation of the kidneys or abnormal kidney function. The guideline's conclusion is that current evidence is not sufficient to say that injectable vaccines cause kidney disease, and that more research is needed.[^abcd-fcv-2025|General recommendations on vaccine type and vaccination protocol]

:::

## Outlook and long-term care

Many acute calicivirus infections resolve within a few days. Pneumonia in kittens, and virulent systemic infection, are the severe end of the spectrum. Reported mortality for the latter is about 30% to 70%.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Acute oral and upper respiratory tract disease][^abcd-fcv-2025|Virulent systemic feline calicivirus infection] Acute herpesvirus disease is especially severe in kittens. Occasional primary pneumonia and viremia can be fatal. Latency afterward is the expected result, and ocular or upper respiratory signs can appear again under stress.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Clinical signs][^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points] Chronic nasal disease that has lasted more than 10 days is not necessarily still "one cold." Polyps, foreign bodies, neoplasia, and causes other than bacteria have to be looked for.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Chronic Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (>10 Days of Duration)]

An association between calicivirus and chronic gingivostomatitis has been seen in several studies, but inoculating the virus into healthy cats did not reproduce the chronic disease, and the amount of virus in the mouth did not line up with how severe the lesions were. A positive result is not the diagnosis of gingivostomatitis.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Feline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS)] The same guideline states that calicivirus does not appear to take part in tooth resorption in cats.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Feline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS)]

## Common misunderstandings

- "A runny, purulent nose means an antibiotic right away." A cat who is bright and still eating can be watched first. Antibiotics are not recommended for serous discharge. Fever, lethargy, or not eating, together with mucopurulent discharge, is when a drug is considered.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (≤10 Days Duration)][^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Suspected Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection]
- "A positive PCR proves that this virus caused this episode." Healthy cats, and cats who were vaccinated recently, can be positive. A negative result also cannot exclude calicivirus.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (≤10 Days Duration)][^abcd-fcv-2025|Diagnosis]
- "Once the cat has recovered there is lifelong immunity, and another vaccine is unnecessary." Immunity after calicivirus infection is not lifelong, and it does not cover every strain. What the vaccine reduces is disease, not infection and shedding.[^abcd-fcv-2025|General recommendations on vaccine type and vaccination protocol]
- "Lysine can prevent relapse." Because evidence of efficacy is lacking, the guideline does not recommend it for preventing re-shedding of herpesvirus.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Treatment]
- "A vaccinated cat cannot get severe systemic calicivirus." In several field outbreaks, vaccine did not provide protection. Experimental studies sometimes saw a milder course. That is not a guarantee in the field.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Vaccination and VS-FC infection]
- "Vaccines cause chronic kidney disease." The studies that exist are not sufficient to establish causation.[^abcd-fcv-2025|General recommendations on vaccine type and vaccination protocol]

## Questions to ask your vet

- Whether this episode looks more like an acute viral infection, or has already gone past 10 days and needs a search for a polyp, a foreign body, or neoplasia. The dividing line uses how long the signs have lasted and the response to treatment, not a single PCR.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Definitions and Causes][^iscaid-uri-2017|Diagnosis of Chronic Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection (>10 Days of Duration)]
- Whether the conditions for an antibiotic are present: fever, lethargy, or not eating, plus mucopurulent discharge. When the discharge is only serous, the guideline does not recommend using one.[^iscaid-uri-2017|Treatment of Suspected Acute Bacterial Upper Respiratory Infection]
- Whether the eye is a herpesvirus ulcer, and whether an antiviral drug is needed, rather than antibiotic drops alone.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points]
- Whether this cat's core vaccines cover herpesvirus and calicivirus, and that a vaccine cannot stop calicivirus infection and shedding.[^abcd-fhv-2026|Key points][^abcd-fcv-2025|General recommendations on vaccine type and vaccination protocol]
- In a multi-cat home, whether the disinfectant is effective against calicivirus. Many common disinfectants are not.[^abcd-fcv-2025|Key points]
